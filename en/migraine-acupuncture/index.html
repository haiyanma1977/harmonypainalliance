Acupuncture for Migraine

Acupuncture and migraine

Migraine can bring throbbing head pain, nausea, and sensitivity to light and sound, and frequent attacks can make it hard to keep up with work, family and sleep. Some people consider acupuncture alongside their regular medical care.

This page summarizes what published research says, what a visit usually involves, and how Harmony Pain Alliance can help you find a licensed acupuncture clinic that focuses on these concerns.

What the research says

Reviews of the research. A 2026 umbrella review of 22 systematic reviews covering 24,635 patients compared acupuncture with standard pharmacological treatment for migraine. It found reductions in pain intensity and in migraine days per month favoring acupuncture, although results varied widely from study to study (high heterogeneity), and a subgroup analysis found the reduction persisted 3 months after acupuncture ended. The results for response to treatment and for adverse events did not reach statistical significance, and the authors rated the quality of the included reviews as low to moderate.1 If you take medication for migraine, please do not stop or change it on your own — talk with your physician first.

Migraine without aura. In a 2017 randomized trial of 249 people aged 18 to 65 with migraine without aura, the frequency of migraine attacks 16 weeks after randomization had decreased by an average of 3.2 attacks in the real acupuncture group, compared with 2.1 in the sham acupuncture group and 1.4 in the waiting-list group, and the authors concluded that real acupuncture may be associated with a long-term reduction in migraine recurrence.2

Episodic migraine. In a 2020 multicentre trial of 150 people with episodic migraine without aura, 20 sessions of manual acupuncture reduced migraine days more than sham acupuncture, and the authors concluded that manual acupuncture was superior to both sham acupuncture and usual care for this type of migraine.3

Monthly migraine days. In a 2026 randomized trial of 120 people with migraine without aura, 12 acupuncture sessions over 4 weeks reduced monthly migraine days more than sham acupuncture (a median difference of 1 day).4

Chronic pain overall. A large analysis of individual data from 20,827 patients in 39 trials covered four chronic pain conditions, one of which was chronic headache — it was not a migraine-only study — and found that, with all four conditions combined, the effect of acupuncture decreased by only about 15% at one year.5

During an attack. A 2023 systematic review of acupuncture for acute migraine attacks (21 randomized trials, 1,926 participants) found that, 2 hours after a session, acupuncture may increase the chance of being headache-free and may improve headache intensity compared with sham acupuncture, but the authors rated the certainty of this evidence as low to very low. Compared with drug treatment, acupuncture may make little to no difference in the rate of headache freedom or headache relief at 2 hours, and the authors concluded that acupuncture "may also be as effective as pharmacological therapy"; again, they rated the certainty of the evidence across outcomes as low to very low.6 If you take medication for migraine, please do not stop or change it on your own — talk with your physician first.

Not every study is positive: in one trial of 480 people, the effect of acupuncture compared with sham acupuncture appeared clinically minor.7 A review of acupuncture for menstrual migraine found no convincing evidence to support its use, and all of the studies it included were too small to give firm answers.8

Research results describe groups of patients, not any one person. Acupuncture is not a substitute for medical evaluation. Seek emergency care right away for a sudden, severe headache, or a headache with fever, stiff neck, confusion, weakness, numbness, or changes in vision or speech; see a physician if your headaches change from your usual pattern.

Lowering how often migraines happen vs. easing an attack

Research on acupuncture for migraine looks at two different goals. Some studies follow how often migraines happen — attacks or migraine days — over weeks or months, as in the 2017 and 2020 trials above. Others look at an attack that is already under way and measure headache relief in the hours after a session, as in the 2023 review above.

If you are thinking about acupuncture, a licensed acupuncture physician can talk with you about which goal fits your situation, alongside the care you already receive from your physician.

How many sessions have been studied

Number of sessions. A 2024 meta-regression of 32 randomized trials (1,562 participants) found a non-linear relationship between the number, frequency and duration of acupuncture sessions and the change in migraine attack frequency. The review reported a decrease in attack frequency after 16 sessions, and significant decreases with 3 sessions a week and after 2 months of acupuncture, after which the improvement gradually leveled off. The authors noted that the reporting quality of the included trials was suboptimal, and most of the studies were rated as having an unclear risk of bias.9

The trials above also used set schedules — for example, 20 sessions of manual acupuncture in the 2020 trial and 12 sessions over 4 weeks in the 2026 trial.3,4 These are research findings, not a recommended plan; how many visits you have, and how often, is something to decide with the clinic.

What to expect at a visit

Most clinics begin with questions about your pain, health history and goals, followed by a physical assessment. Treatment uses very thin, single-use sterile needles; some clinics also use electroacupuncture, cupping or other techniques, depending on your condition. Tell your practitioner about any medical conditions and medications, especially blood thinners.

In Florida, acupuncture is provided by Acupuncture Physicians (AP) licensed by the Florida Department of Health. Insurance coverage depends on your plan and on the clinic — ask the clinic and your insurer before your first visit.
